Reading the color of water from space

I am an aquatic remote sensing scientist. I combine satellite observations with field measurements to understand how lakes, reservoirs, rivers and coastal waters change, and what drives that change.

As a Postdoctoral Associate in the Environmental Change & Sustainability Lab at Indiana University Bloomington, I study how solar panels installed on water affect the lakes and reservoirs that host them, using a decade of satellite imagery to compare water surface temperature and a chlorophyll-a index before and after installation. Before that, as a FAPESP postdoctoral fellow at the University of São Paulo, I combined bio-optical field measurements with satellite water-color imagery to estimate dissolved organic carbon and CO₂ partial pressure in the Amazon River, from the river to its ocean plume.

After a B.S. in Biology, I earned my M.Sc. and Ph.D. in Remote Sensing at Brazil’s National Institute for Space Research (INPE). Much of my work starts in the field: eight campaigns on the lower Amazon River, monthly sampling at a coastal time-series station, oceanographic cruises along the Brazilian coast, and reservoir surveys. Satellite signals from water are easy to get, but easy to misread without understanding the optical and biological processes behind them. Field measurements are how I build that understanding.

Research

Solar panels on water

Global, 2025 to present

Solar panels are being installed on lakes, reservoirs and ponds worldwide, but no inventory resolved individual installations, and single-site studies disagree about their effects. I built a global inventory of 3,277 aquatic photovoltaic installations on 2,545 host water bodies in 36 countries. Most of these water bodies are small, artificial and missing from global lake databases. I use the inventory with a decade of Landsat and Sentinel-2 imagery to compare each lake before and after installation against nearby lakes without panels, measuring changes in water temperature and algae.

Carbon and color in the Amazon River Continuum

Lower Amazon River to the Atlantic plume, 2014 to 2024

The Amazon carries carbon from land to the atmosphere and the ocean. I use the optical properties of dissolved organic matter, measured in the field and from satellites such as Sentinel-3 OLCI and PACE OCI, to estimate dissolved organic carbon, CO₂ partial pressure and chlorophyll-a along the river and its plume. This work includes classifying the river’s optical water types and testing atmospheric correction methods for its turbid waters.

Water color of Amazonian fishponds

Brazilian Amazon, 2022 to present

Fish farming is expanding quickly in the Brazilian Amazon, mostly in small earthen ponds that satellites rarely resolve. As part of a FAPESP project on the carbon footprint and impacts of aquaculture expansion, we followed experimental fishponds at Embrapa Fisheries and Aquaculture through a full production cycle and showed that pond reflectance, simulated for PlanetScope satellites, can tell ponds stocked with fish from inactive ones. We also compared field, satellite and smartphone measurements of pond color, and are now using Sentinel-2 time series of a chlorophyll-a index to distinguish fishponds from other water bodies across a major fish-farming region in Rondônia. The same project also supported our study of the land use and carbon emissions of aquaculture expansion in the Amazon, published in Science Advances.

Bio-optics of reservoirs and coastal waters

Brazil and Chile, 2007 to 2024

My earliest work characterized the optical behaviour of Manso Reservoir in central Brazil. Since then I have worked on the bio-optical properties of coastal waters in southeastern Brazil and northern Patagonia, and contributed to BRAZA, an open bio-optical database for Brazilian coastal and inland waters.

Data and tools

AqPV global inventory

A geospatial database of 3,277 aquatic photovoltaic installations on 2,545 host water bodies in 36 countries, each mapped individually, with installation dates and attributes of the structure and its host water body. Openly available on Zenodo; the accompanying data paper is in preparation for Scientific Data. Get the dataset.

AqPV web tool

An interactive platform to explore aquatic solar installations and the water-quality and temperature indices of their host lakes. Built by Freny Reji, an M.S. student I co-mentored at Indiana University. Open the web tool.

BRAZA bio-optical database

An open database of in situ bio-optical measurements for Brazilian coastal and inland waters, to which I contributed field data. Get the dataset or read the data paper.

Teaching and mentoring

I teach remote sensing as a way of seeing change on Earth with your own eyes. In my introductory lecture at Indiana University, students explore NASA Worldview and Google Earth Timelapse to find changes in landscapes and water bodies themselves. In the field, I have always trained new students in bio-optical measurements and remote sensing.
